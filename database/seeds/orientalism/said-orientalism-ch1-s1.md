# Orientalism — Chapter 1, Section I: “Knowing the Oriental”

### A meticulous, concept-by-concept reading (pp. 31–48)

> Page anchors in `((n))` match the book’s pagination. Quotations are short and used only to fix Said’s wording; everything else is paraphrase and commentary.

**Why this section exists.** Section I sets up the entire book by showing Orientalism *as practice before theory*. Said deliberately opens a study of a scholarly tradition with two **administrators**, not scholars — Balfour and Cromer — to prove that “knowing” the Orient and *ruling* it are one continuous act. The whole section turns on the two “Baconian themes”: **knowledge and power**.

-----

## 0. Epigraph — Fourier, *Description de l’Égypte* (1809)

Said opens with Jean-Baptiste-Joseph Fourier praising the “restless and ambitious genius of the Europeans… impatient to employ the new instruments of their power.” The epigraph is chosen because it fuses *intellect* (“genius”) with *domination* (“instruments of power”) — the book’s thesis in one sentence, and it comes from Napoleon’s scholarly monument on Egypt (foreshadowing Section III).

-----

## 1. The Balfour speech, June 13, 1910 ( ((31))–((35)) )

**The occasion.** Balfour lectures the Commons on “the problems with which we have to deal in Egypt,” insisting they “belong to a wholly different category” than the Isle of Wight or Yorkshire. → The first move is to mark Egypt as *categorically other*.

**His authority.** Said lists Balfour’s credentials (former PM, secretary for Scotland/Ireland, witness to Afghan/Zulu wars, the 1882 occupation, Omdurman, the Boer War, etc.). → Authority to *speak about* the Orient is itself part of the apparatus.

**Why so didactic.** Some members questioned “England in Egypt” (Milner’s 1892 phrase) now that Egyptian nationalism was rising. Balfour speaks *to reassure a wavering home audience*.

**Robertson’s challenge.** Balfour repeats J. M. Robertson’s question: “What right have you to take up these airs of superiority with regard to people whom you choose to call Oriental?” → Note “choose to call” — the category is an *imposition*, which Balfour will not actually examine.

**“Oriental” is canonical.** The word needs no defense: used by Chaucer, Mandeville, Shakespeare, Dryden, Pope, Byron; even Marx used it. → The vocabulary is so embedded it “called for no comment whatever.”

**The disavowal.** “I take up no attitude of superiority” — yet he immediately asserts British “supremacy over great races.” → Said’s point: the *denial* of superiority while *enacting* it is structural, not hypocrisy he needs to expose.

**Knowledge as the ground of rule.** “We know the civilization of Egypt better than… any other country.” Supremacy is tied to **knowledge**, not first to guns or money.

**The two Baconian themes — knowledge and power — unpacked:**

- Knowledge = surveying a civilization “from its origins to its prime to its decline,” i.e. *rising above self into the foreign and distant*.
- The object of such knowledge is “inherently vulnerable to scrutiny” and treated as a stable “fact” — even “ontologically stable.”
- **To have such knowledge is to dominate it**; authority means “for ‘us’ to deny autonomy to ‘it’.”
- The killer line Said extracts: **“British knowledge of Egypt is Egypt for Balfour.”** The known thing is *replaced by* the knowledge of it.
- Superiority/inferiority become “petty” questions next to the burden of knowledge — Balfour never denies the hierarchy, he *takes it for granted*.

**Self-government.** Balfour: look through “the whole history of the Orientals” and “you never find traces of self-government.” Their “great centuries” were all “under despotisms.” → The Oriental is *constitutively* incapable of governing himself; greatness only ever happened under absolutism.

**Is British rule good for them?** Yes — they get “far better government than… they ever had before,” a benefit “to them” and “to the whole of the civilised West.” “We are in Egypt… for their sake; we are there also for the sake of Europe at large.”

**No consent, no voice.** Balfour offers no evidence Egyptians appreciate this, and it never occurs to him to “let the Egyptian speak for himself.” Any Egyptian who would speak is pre-labeled “the agitator who wishes to raise difficulties.”

**The practical part.** England “exports our very best.” The administrators work “amidst tens of thousands” of a different creed/race; their power rests on the natives’ “instinctive feeling” that the home government backs them. Remove that backing and “those populations lose all that sense of order which is the very basis of their civilisation.”

**The circular syllogism (Said’s reconstruction).** England knows Egypt → Egypt *is* what England knows → England knows Egypt cannot self-govern → England occupies Egypt → for the Egyptians, “Egypt is what England has occupied and now governs” → occupation becomes “the very basis” of Egyptian civilization → “Egypt requires, indeed insists upon, British occupation.” → The argument *manufactures the reality that then justifies it.*

**Balfour as ventriloquist.** As “a rhetorical performance,” Balfour “plays the part of, and represents, a variety of characters”:

- “the English” — the lordly “we”;
- “the civilised world, the West,” and the small corps of colonial officials;
- and even **the Orientals** — he speaks for them because “they after all speak another language,” and were they asked, they would “uselessly confirm” that they are “a subject race, dominated by a race that knows them… better than they could possibly know themselves.”
  → Self-representation is structurally impossible: the Oriental’s imagined speech only ratifies his subjugation.

**Egypt as exemplary.** Egypt “was not just another colony”: it was “the vindication of Western imperialism,” “an almost academic example of Oriental backwardness,” destined to become “the triumph of English knowledge and power.”

## 2. Cromer — Orientalism at the administrator’s desk ( ((35))–((38)) )

**Who he is.** Evelyn Baring, “Over-baring,” Egypt’s “master” from 1882 (when Britain crushed Colonel Arabi’s nationalist revolt) to 1907. Balfour championed his £50,000 retirement prize: “Cromer made Egypt.” Balfour claims Cromer raised Egypt to stand “absolutely alone in its prosperity, financial and moral” — though, Said notes drily, *how* “moral prosperity” was measured “Balfour did not venture to say.”

**A theory that “worked.”** The most important thing about the governing theory was “that it worked, and worked staggeringly well.” Reduced to its simplest form: “There are Westerners, and there are Orientals. The former dominate; the latter must be dominated.” → Its power lies in being “clear… precise… easy to grasp.”

**Not personal viciousness.** That Balfour and Cromer could “strip humanity down to such ruthless cultural and racial essences” reflects not their nastiness but how “streamlined” and “effective” the doctrine had become.

**Cromer vs Balfour.** Balfour’s “Orientals” pretend to “objective universality”; Cromer speaks of the people he actually ruled. Balfour’s “Orientals” = Cromer’s **“subject races,”** the topic of his 1908 *Edinburgh Review* essay “The Government of Subject Races.”

**The knowledge–power dialectic.** “Knowledge gives power, more power requires more knowledge, and so on in an increasingly profitable dialectic of information and control.”

**Rule by understanding “limitations.”** Since the Oriental supposedly ignores logic, one should not force “ultrascientific measures” on him but “understand his limitations” and find a “stronger bond of union” in “the contentment of the subject race.” Imperial might lurks behind everything, “more effective for its refined understanding and infrequent use.” The empire must be “wise,” tempering “cupidity with selflessness.”

**Wards, not citizens.** Subject peoples are “in statu pupillari” (permanent pupils/wards). Decisions are made “by the light of Western knowledge… what we conscientiously think is best for the subject race.” The hoped-for result is a “cosmopolitan allegiance” grounded in gratitude — capped by the sneering image of the “Central African savage” learning to praise the British official “who denies him gin but gives him justice.”

**Against nationalism.** Cromer rejects “narrow nationalism” for “an enlarged cosmopolitanism.” Subject races “did not have it in them to know what was good for them.”

**The Platonic essence.** Managing Orientals “was almost everywhere nearly the same” — “because Orientals were almost everywhere nearly the same.” → The tautology *is* the doctrine: the Oriental is a fixed essence any Orientalist “might examine, understand, and expose.”

## 3. The stereotype catalog — *Modern Egypt*, ch. 34 ( ((38))–((39)) )

Cromer’s “personal canon of Orientalist wisdom,” quoted by Said to show the *content* of the essence:

- Lyall’s maxim: “Accuracy is abhorrent to the Oriental mind.”
- **The European:** “a close reasoner,” “a natural logician,” “sceptical,” his intelligence “works like a piece of mechanism.”
- **The Oriental:** “wanting in symmetry,” reasoning “of the most slipshod description,” contradicts himself, “breaks down under the mildest process of cross-examination.”
- Further: gullible, “devoid of energy and initiative,” prone to “fulsome flattery,” intrigue, cunning, cruelty to animals; even *unable to walk on roads or pavements*; “inveterate liars,” “lethargic and suspicious.”

**Borrowed authority.** Cromer cites the “orthodox Orientalist authorities” **Renan and Volney** to explain *why* Orientals are this way. → Administrator leans on scholar; the loop closes.

**The tautology of guilt.** The Egyptian “breaking under cross-examination” is found *guilty* — and “the crime was that the Oriental was an Oriental.” Any deviation from the supposed norm is read as “unnatural”; thus Cromer dismisses Egyptian nationalism as “an entirely novel idea” and “a plant of exotic rather than of indigenous growth.”

## 4. The theoretical synthesis ( ((39))–((41)) )

- **Don’t underestimate the archive.** Behind Balfour and Cromer is a “reservoir of accredited knowledge,” the “codes of Orientalist orthodoxy.”
- **The decisive claim — rule justified *in advance*:** “To say simply that Orientalism was a rationalization of colonial rule is to ignore the extent to which colonial rule was justified in advance by Orientalism, rather than after the fact.” → Knowledge precedes and *licenses* conquest.
- **Two long-running features (since the mid-18th c.):**
1. a “growing systematic knowledge” of the Orient, reinforced by colonial contact and the new sciences (ethnology, comparative anatomy, philology, history), plus a literature by “novelists, poets, translators, and gifted travelers”;
1. Europe “always in a position of strength, not to say domination” — “There is no way of putting this euphemistically.”
- **The fixed binary:** Oriental = “irrational, depraved (fallen), childlike, ‘different’”; European = “rational, virtuous, mature, ‘normal’.”
- **Knowledge creates the Orient.** The Oriental’s world has its own apparent coherence, but its “intelligibility and identity” came not from Orientals but from “the whole complex series of knowledgeable manipulations by which the Orient was identified by the West.” → “Knowledge of the Orient, because generated out of strength, in a sense creates the Orient.”
- **The four dominating frameworks.** In Balfour’s and Cromer’s language the Oriental is:
  - “something one judges” — *as in a court of law*;
  - “something one studies and depicts” — *as in a curriculum*;
  - “something one disciplines” — *as in a school or prison*;
  - “something one illustrates” — *as in a zoological manual*.
    → He is “contained and represented by dominating frameworks.”
- **“Cultural strength.”** Said names his real object — Orientalism as “an exercise of cultural strength” — but declines to generalize yet (the seed of his later use of Gramsci’s *hegemony*).
- **The section’s working definition:** “Orientalism… is knowledge of the Orient that places things Oriental in class, court, prison, or manual for scrutiny, study, judgment, discipline, or governing.”

## 5. Orientalism as archive, and its history ( ((41))–((43)) )

- **An inherited vocabulary.** Balfour and Cromer could speak as they did because “a still earlier tradition” supplied “a vocabulary, imagery, rhetoric, and figures.”
- **The scale of empire.** The rise of Orientalism “coincides exactly” with European expansion: from **1815 to 1914 colonial dominion grew from about 35% to about 85% of the earth’s surface.**
- **British–French rivalry.** “Allies and partners in some things,” “hostile rivals” in others; their possessions overlapped and were fought over. The **Near Orient** — “the lands of the Arab Near East, where Islam was supposed to define cultural and racial characteristics” — was the zone of greatest intensity.
- **Salisbury’s logic (1881).** Faced with a meddling ally, “You may renounce — or monopolize — or share.” Renouncing meant France across the road to India; monopolizing risked war; “so we resolved to share.” → What they *shared* was not only land and profit but “the kind of intellectual power I have been calling Orientalism.”
- **Orientalism as a library/archive.** “A library or archive of information commonly… held,” bound together by “a family of ideas” and a “unifying set of values.” These ideas gave Orientals “a mentality, a genealogy, an atmosphere,” and let Europeans see them as “a phenomenon possessing regular characteristics.”
- **Constraints, not just doctrine.** Orientalism “is better grasped as a set of constraints upon and limitations of thought than… as a positive doctrine.” It “deepened and even hardened” the West-superior/Orient-inferior distinction.
- **The “young Raj.”** A telling refinement: Britain retired administrators at 55, so “no Oriental was ever allowed to see a Westerner as he aged and degenerated,” and no Westerner saw himself “mirrored in the eyes of the subject race” as anything but “a vigorous, rational, ever-alert young Raj.”

**Three forms Orientalist ideas took (19th–20th c.):**

1. **Inherited literature → an “Oriental renaissance”** (Edgar Quinet’s phrase). Modern Orientalism begins late-18th/early-19th c., fed by newly translated texts (Sanskrit, Zend, Arabic). Its keynote: **Napoleon’s invasion of Egypt (1798)** — “the very model of a truly scientific appropriation of one culture by another”; the *Description de l’Égypte* makes Egypt “the live province, the laboratory, the theater” of Western knowledge.
1. **Modernized knowledge.** Renan’s 1848 Semitic linguistics borrowed authority from “comparative grammar, comparative anatomy, and racial theory.” This gave prestige but left Orientalism “vulnerable… to modish… currents” — “imperialism, positivism, utopianism, historicism, Darwinism, racism, Freudianism, Marxism, Spenglerism.” It acquired “paradigms,” learned societies (Société asiatique, Royal Asiatic Society, Deutsche Morgenländische Gesellschaft, American Oriental Society), professorships, and periodicals (the *Fundgraben des Orients*, 1809).
1. **Limits on thought.** “Even the most imaginative writers” — Flaubert, Nerval, Scott — “were constrained in what they could either experience of or say about the Orient.” Orientalism is “ultimately a political vision of reality” enforcing the split between “the familiar (Europe, the West, ‘us’)” and “the strange (the Orient, the East, ‘them’).” Vision and material reality “propped each other up.” The Westerner enjoyed a “freedom of intercourse”: he could “penetrate,” “wrestle with,” and “give shape and meaning to the great Asiatic mystery” (Disraeli). Said’s verdict: this reality is “both antihuman and persistent.”

## 6. “How did and does Orientalism work?” — Cromer’s machine ( ((44))–((45)) )

- In “The Government of Subject Races,” Cromer contrasts the **“local agent”** (specialist knowledge + Anglo-Saxon individuality) with the **“central authority”** in London that ensures “the harmonious working of the different parts of the machine.”
- Said reads this as the architecture of power: a “seat of power in the West,” with “a great embracing machine” radiating eastward. What the East feeds in — “human material, material wealth, knowledge” — is “processed by the machine, then converted into more power.”
- The specialist performs “the immediate translation of mere Oriental matter into useful substance”: the person becomes “a subject race,” “an example of an ‘Oriental’ mentality,” for “the enhancement of the ‘authority’ at home.”
- The general principle: **“the management of knowledge by society”** — knowledge regulated first by the specialist’s local concerns, then by “the general concerns of a social system of authority.”
- **Pope’s line, twisted.** Pope said “the proper study of mankind is Man” — meaning *all* men, including “the poor Indian.” Cromer’s “the proper study is *also* man” singles Orientals out as the object of study. → “The proper study… of Orientals is Orientalism.”
- **Kipling’s “mock chain of being.”** Mule → driver → sergeant → lieutenant → captain → major → colonel → brigadier → general → Viceroy → “the servant of the Empress.” → An “order of sovereignty… set up from East to West.”

-----

## 7. The central intellectual problem ( ((45))–((46)) )

Said widens out to the question the whole book confronts:

> “Can one divide human reality… into clearly different cultures, histories, traditions, societies, even races, and survive the consequences humanly?”

- “Surviving the consequences humanly” = avoiding “the hostility expressed by the division… of men into ‘us’ (Westerners) and ‘they’ (Orientals).”
- Such divisions are “generalities” historically used “towards not especially admirable ends.”
- Using “Oriental” and “Western” as both starting and ending points “polarizes the distinction — the Oriental becomes more Oriental, the Westerner more Western” — and “limits the human encounter between different cultures.”
- The result: “the sense of Western power over the Orient is taken for granted as having the status of scientific truth.”

-----

## 8. Orientalism is not over: two contemporary cases ( ((46))–((48)) )

Said insists this is a *living* discourse, not a Victorian relic.

**(a) Henry Kissinger, “Domestic Structure and Foreign Policy.”**

- Kissinger proceeds by what linguists call **binary opposition** (prophetic vs political; developed vs developing).
- The West “is deeply committed to the notion that the real world is external to the observer,” that “knowledge consists of recording and classifying data.” The proof is “the Newtonian revolution,” which “has not taken place in the developing world,” where the real world is held to be “almost completely internal to the observer.” Hence “they never went through the process of discovering it.”
- Said: Kissinger needn’t cite Lyall — “We had our Newtonian revolution; they didn’t” is taken as self-evident. “The lines are drawn in much the same way… as Balfour and Cromer drew them.”
- Difference from Cromer: Kissinger *respects* the pre-Newtonian view for its “flexibility” amid “revolutionary turmoil,” and urges constructing “an international order before a crisis imposes it” — i.e., a way “the developing world can be contained.” → A modern echo of “Cromer’s vision of a harmoniously working machine.”
- His vocabulary (“prophetic,” “accuracy,” “internal,” “empirical reality,” “order”) “is not value-free”: it codes the West as virtuous/familiar and the Other as menacing/disorderly. Culture-difference becomes “a battlefront,” inviting the West to “control, contain, and otherwise govern… the Other.”

**(b) Harold W. Glidden, “The Arab World” (*American Journal of Psychiatry*, Feb. 1972).**

- A retired State Department intelligence officer offers “a four-page, double-columned psychological portrait of over 100 million people” across “1,300 years,” citing “exactly four sources.”
- It claims to reveal “the inner workings of Arab behavior” — “aberrant” to us, “normal” for Arabs: Arabs stress conformity; inhabit a “shame culture” of prestige and clientage; “can function only in conflict situations”; base prestige “solely on the ability to dominate others”; make “a virtue of revenge” (and thus “Islam itself”). → A pseudo-scientific, psychiatric reincarnation of Cromer’s catalog — Orientalism wearing a 20th-century lab coat.

## One-paragraph synthesis

Section I argues that **Orientalism is power-knowledge in operation**: through Balfour and Cromer, knowing the Orient *is* dominating it; the Oriental is a fixed essence denied a voice, justified for rule *before* the fact; the discourse is a vast self-confirming **archive** that grew alongside empire (35%→85% of the globe), that constrains even great writers, that runs like Cromer’s “embracing machine,” and that — via Kissinger and Glidden — still structures Western thought about the Other today. The deep wound Said leaves open: whether humanity can divide itself into “us” and “them” and “survive the consequences humanly.”

-----

## A critical footnote (read it, don’t just absorb it)

Said builds his case on unusually candid imperialists and the occasional crude essay (Glidden), then treats them as representative of an entire scholarly field. Critics such as **Bernard Lewis** and **Robert Irwin** argue this conflates administrators and propagandists with serious philologists, and selects the most damning specimens; defenders reply that the *shared discourse* between statecraft and scholarship is exactly Said’s point. Hold that tension as we move to **Section II — Imaginative Geography**, where Said turns from the colonial office to the deep literary and cultural roots of the East/West line.

# PART II — DEEPER LAYERS

## 9. The theoretical engines under Section I

Said never stops to lecture on his sources, but four thinkers are driving every move.

**Foucault — discourse and power/knowledge.** When Said says Balfour and Cromer *speak the discourse*, he means (after Foucault) that Orientalism is a system of statements with its own “rules of formation”: what can be said about the Orient, by whom, with what authority. Three Foucauldian mechanisms are visible in Section I:

- **Power produces, it doesn’t only repress.** The line “British knowledge of Egypt *is* Egypt” is the productivity of power/knowledge: the discourse *makes* the object it claims to find.
- **The author is a function, not an origin.** Balfour and Cromer are interchangeable mouthpieces; the *archive* speaks through them, which is why two different men say nearly identical things.
- **The statement, not the sentence.** What matters is not Cromer’s prose but the *regularity* — the same Oriental reappears in Balfour, Cromer, Kissinger, Glidden across 60 years.

**Gramsci — hegemony and “cultural strength.”** Said flags “cultural strength” and refuses to define it yet; this is his on-ramp to Gramsci, fully used later. In Section I the seeds are:

- **Consent, not just coercion.** Cromer’s preference to rule through “the contentment of the subject race,” with force held “in reserve,” is hegemony in miniature — domination that prefers to look like leadership.
- **Civil vs political society.** The “embracing machine” is the *state* (political society); the learned societies, professorships and periodicals are *civil society* — and Orientalism saturates both.
- **Positional superiority** (named in §II but operating here): the Westerner never loses “the relative upper hand,” whatever stance he takes.

**Vico — “men make their own history.”** Balfour calls the Oriental’s incapacity “the fact.” Said’s Vichian counter (developed in the Introduction) is that such “facts” are *made*, not found — geography and the Orient included. Section I is the demonstration: the “fact” of Egyptian incapacity is manufactured by the very knowledge that claims merely to report it.

**Bacon — “knowledge is power.”** Said calls the speech’s two themes “Baconian” deliberately. Bacon fused *scientia* and *potentia*; Balfour’s syllogism is that fusion turned into statecraft — to map a civilization “origin → prime → decline” is already to hold it.

> **The famous tension to keep in view:** Foucault dissolves the individual author into anonymous discourse; Said simultaneously wants to credit *individual* genius (Lane, Flaubert, Renan) and to mount a *humanist* protest against the discourse. He is therefore half-Foucauldian. Critics (James Clifford, Aijaz Ahmad) argue this is incoherent — you cannot both say “the discourse determines everything” and “yet these gifted men chose their words.” Whether it is a contradiction or a productive double vision is a real, open question — and Section I is where the strain first shows.

## 10. The rhetoric, read as machinery

Said is a literary critic; Section I is, underneath, a close *rhetorical* analysis. The deeper layer is to see the devices as a typification *technology*:

- **The imperial pronoun.** Watch “we/us” slide its referent: first “the English,” then “the civilised world,” then “everyone reasonable.” The Oriental is always “it”/“they,” never “we.” Grammar does the othering before any argument does.
- **The forensic lexicon.** “Facts,” “case,” “evidence,” “cross-examination,” “guilty.” The Oriental is put *on trial* in a court whose rules he didn’t write and whose language he doesn’t speak — and is convicted of being himself. The juridical vocabulary is how domination disguises itself as objectivity.
- **The tense of the essence.** Oriental greatness is always *past tense* (“their great moments were in the past”); Oriental defects are an *eternal present*. The essence is timeless, so evidence can never refute it — only confirm it.
- **The catalogue.** Cromer’s litany (illogical, lethargic, liar, can’t use a pavement…) is not stray prejudice; the *list form itself* converts a hundred million people into a fixed type. Enumeration manufactures the “regular characteristics.”
- **Citation as laundering.** Cromer → Lyall → Renan/Volney: authority circulates in a closed ring, each citation “confirming” the next, none ever touching an actual Egyptian. The footnote is the discourse certifying itself.
- **Agency by passive voice.** “The Orient *was Orientalized*” — the doer hides; the made thing looks given. Said’s whole method is to restore the missing subject of that sentence: *who* did the Orientalizing, and from what position of strength.

## 11. History behind the names (what Said is counting on you to feel)

- **Balfour is not a random imperialist.** Seven years after this 1910 speech he signs the **Balfour Declaration (1917)**, promising a national home in Palestine — speaking *for* the future of a land whose existing inhabitants he barely consults. For Said, a Palestinian, choosing Balfour as the book’s opening voice is pointed: the 1910 speech and the 1917 Declaration are the *same gesture* of authoritative speaking-for.
- **Cromer’s “moral prosperity” has a real face.** The **Dinshaway incident (1906)** — savage floggings and hangings of Egyptian villagers after an altercation with British officers over pigeon-shooting — happened on Cromer’s watch and helped ignite the nationalism he then dismissed as “exotic.” His “veiled protectorate” ran Egypt’s economy for cotton and debt service. Balfour’s “absolutely alone in its prosperity, financial and moral” should be read against Dinshaway.
- **The imperial backdrop Balfour name-drops.** The **Urabi (Arabi) revolt** and the 1882 **bombardment of Alexandria** → occupation; **Gordon at Khartoum (1885)**, **Omdurman (1898)**, **Fashoda (1898)** — the Anglo-French near-collision Salisbury resolves with “share.” These are the “overseas crises” that authorize Balfour’s voice.
- **Milner’s *England in Egypt* (1892)** supplied the ideology of benevolent indefinite occupation Balfour is defending.
- **Kissinger and Glidden are Cold-War Orientalism.** Kissinger’s essay belongs to late-1960s détente strategising; Glidden’s psychiatric “Arab World” grows out of the **area-studies / intelligence nexus** — exactly the “instrumental” scholarship Said (citing Chomsky in the Introduction) says pretends to be objective while serving state power.

## 13. The strongest objections (so you can argue with the book, not just absorb it)

1. **Administrators ≠ scholars (Lewis, Irwin).** Section I’s evidence is two pro-consuls and a propaganda essay; Lewis and Irwin argue real philology (Sacy, Nöldeke, Goldziher) was often critical, self-correcting, and not in colonial service. Said’s reply is built into the design: the *point* is that statecraft and scholarship share one discourse — but the reader should weigh whether the sample is stacked.
1. **He essentialises “the West” (Sadik al-’Azm, “Orientalism in Reverse”).** To make “Europe” a single will that always dominates is arguably to do to the Occident exactly what Orientalists did to the Orient — the mirror-image error.
1. **The colonised have no voice in his account either (Ahmad).** Orientals appear in Section I only as objects of others’ speech; the book criticises that silencing yet largely reproduces it, offering little Eastern agency or counter-discourse.
1. **Geography of omission.** German and Hungarian Orientalism (no colonies; Goldziher a sympathetic giant) sit awkwardly with a thesis tying the field so tightly to British/French empire.
1. **Does he deny a knowable Orient?** Said says no (Introduction, qualification one), but the relentless “knowledge creates the Orient” can read as anti-realism — a tension the *Afterword* tries to settle.

## 14. Mini-glossary (Section I usage)

- **Discourse** — a rule-governed system of statements that produces its object; here, the Orient.
- **Power/knowledge** — the claim that knowing and dominating are one operation, not two.
- **Positional superiority** — the structural upper hand the Westerner keeps in every possible relation to the Orient.
- **Cultural strength / hegemony** — leadership-by-consent that makes domination feel like the natural order.
- **Representation / ventriloquism** — speaking *for* the Oriental so that he need never (and can never) speak for himself.
- **Platonic essence / essentialism** — treating “the Oriental” as a single, timeless, unchanging type.
- **The archive / library** — the shared store of Orientalist “facts” that authors draw on and replenish.
- **Imaginative geography** *(named in §II, operating here)* — the arbitrary, mind-made line dividing “our” space from “theirs.”

## 15. Source apparatus — the documentary base of Section I

- **Evelyn Baring, Lord Cromer**, *Modern Egypt* (Macmillan, 1908), esp. vol. 2 — the stereotype catalogue (ch. 34); also *Ancient and Modern Imperialism* (1910).
- **Cromer**, “The Government of Subject Races,” *Edinburgh Review* (Jan. 1908).
- **Rudyard Kipling** — the “chain of command” passage; **Alexander Pope** — “the proper study of mankind is Man”; **Disraeli** — “the great Asiatic mystery.”
- **Edgar Quinet**, *Le Génie des religions* — the “Oriental renaissance.”
